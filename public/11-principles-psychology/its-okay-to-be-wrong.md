# It's okay to be wrong

> “It’s okay to be wrong; it’s not okay to stay wrong.”

This line is best treated as an old trading maxim rather than a quotation with a
definitively proven author. It is often attributed to investor Martin “Marty”
Zweig, sometimes in the stronger form, “It’s unforgivable to stay wrong.” Barry
Ritholtz instead describes it as an [old traders’
expression](https://ritholtz.com/2013/04/the-fine-art-of-being-worng/). Closely
related versions have circulated among professional traders for decades.

The attribution is uncertain. The principle is not.

This page is a decision principle, not a claim that every adverse move proves the thesis wrong. The written invalidation condition remains the authority.

## Wrong versus staying wrong

**Being wrong** is a forecasting error. Markets are probabilistic, so a valid
decision process will still produce losing trades. No trader controls which
individual thesis succeeds.

**Staying wrong** is a process error. It happens when the market invalidates the
thesis and the trader remains exposed, adds risk, or searches for reasons to
ignore the new evidence.

The first loss is a cost of uncertainty. The additional loss is often a cost of
ego, hope, or poor risk discipline.

{% hint style="info" %}
Being wrong is a cost of participating. Staying wrong after invalidation is a
failure of discipline.
{% endhint %}

## Why traders stay wrong

Several predictable biases make correction difficult:

- **Confirmation bias:** seeking evidence that supports the position while
  dismissing evidence against it. The [APA Dictionary of
  Psychology](https://dictionary.apa.org/confirmation-bias) defines the bias in
  these terms.
- **Belief perseverance:** maintaining a conclusion after the evidence that
  created it has been discredited. Classic experiments found that initial
  impressions could survive explicit correction ([Ross, Lepper, and Hubbard,
  1975](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.32.5.880)).
- **Escalation of commitment:** investing more in a failing decision because
  abandoning it would force the original cost to be recognized as a loss. This
  is closely related to the sunk-cost effect ([Martens and Orzen,
  2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2021.103811)).
- **Identity protection:** treating the position as proof of competence. New
  information then feels like a personal threat rather than an update to the
  trade.

## Make the maxim operational

Before entering a trade:

1. State the thesis in observable terms.
2. Define what price should do if the thesis is correct.
3. Define the event that would invalidate it.
4. Size the trade so accepting that invalidation is tolerable.
5. Exit when invalidated without waiting for emotional certainty.
6. Re-enter only if new evidence creates a new thesis.

This separates the trade from the trader. Exiting does not mean the trader is
incapable; it means the original conditions no longer exist.

## Do not confuse noise with invalidation

The maxim does not mean exiting whenever price moves against the position.
Adverse movement can remain consistent with the thesis. Invalidation is a
specific observation that shows the expected auction behavior is no longer
present.

Without a predefined invalidation condition, “do not stay wrong” can become an
excuse for emotional overreaction. The trader may repeatedly abandon valid
positions because ordinary volatility feels like proof of failure.

{% hint style="warning" %}
Do not decide whether the trade is wrong from unrealized P&L. Decide from the
market condition that authorized the trade and the evidence that condition has
failed.
{% endhint %}

## The rule

You do not control whether a thesis is wrong. You control how long and how much
you remain exposed after the market invalidates it.

Define the evidence first. Accept the answer when it arrives.
